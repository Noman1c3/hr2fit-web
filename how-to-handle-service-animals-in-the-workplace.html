How to Handle Service Animals in the Workplace

Dr. Cindy Cushman Founder & Owner, HR2fit • February 27, 2024 • 5 min read

Under Title I of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), employers with 15 or more employees must provide reasonable accommodations to qualified individuals with disabilities—and in many cases, this includes accommodating service animals in the workplace. State and local laws may apply even to smaller employers.

For small and mid-sized employers without a dedicated in-house legal team, managing service animal requests requires careful balancing of employee rights, workplace safety, and coworker health.

What Qualifies as a Service Animal?

Under the ADA, a service animal is defined specifically as a dog (or in some cases miniature horse) individually trained to do work or perform tasks for the benefit of an individual with a disability. This includes guiding visually impaired individuals, alerting deaf individuals, pulling wheelchairs, or alerting handlers to impending medical crises.

Emotional support animals (ESAs) or therapy animals—which provide comfort simply by being present—do not automatically qualify as service animals under public ADA titles, but employers must still evaluate them as potential reasonable accommodations under Title I unless doing so causes undue hardship.

The Interactive Accommodation Process

When an employee requests to bring a service animal to work, employers should engage in an interactive dialogue:

  • Ask permissible questions: If the disability or need is not obvious, employers may request medical documentation confirming the disability and the animal's functional assistance.
  • Verify behavior & training: Employers may require evidence that the animal is housebroken, well-mannered, vaccinated, and does not pose a direct threat to workplace health or safety.
  • Provide reasonable support: Accommodations include designated outdoor relief areas, flexible break schedules for animal care, and workstation adjustments.

Handling Coworker Allergies & Phobias

If a coworker has severe dog allergies or phobias, the employer must accommodate both employees. Solutions include physical distance between workstations, air purifiers, dander-reducing grooming products, or flexible telework arrangements. Employers must never disclose an employee's underlying medical condition to coworkers.
