What Every Massachusetts Employer Needs to Know About the Sick Time Law

Dr. Cindy Cushman Founder & Owner, HR2fit • February 22, 2024 • 7 min read

The Massachusetts Earned Sick Time Law (M.G.L. c. 149, § 148C) protects the jobs and wages of workers across the Commonwealth who need time off for their own medical care, to care for a family member, or to address the effects of domestic violence. Nearly every private employer in Massachusetts is subject to its provisions.

Understanding accrual rates, paid versus unpaid thresholds, and documentation rules is crucial to avoiding severe treble damages and state enforcement actions.

Which Employers Must Provide Paid vs. Unpaid Sick Time?

The law applies to all private employers, but compensation rules depend on employer headcount:

  • 11 or more employees: Employers must provide paid earned sick time.
  • Fewer than 11 employees: Employers must still provide earned sick time, but it may be unpaid. All other job protections still apply.

To calculate headcount, employers must count all employees—including full-time, part-time, temporary, and seasonal workers across all company locations inside or outside Massachusetts—during the preceding benefit year.

Accrual Rates & Usage Limits

Employees earn 1 hour of sick time for every 30 hours worked, up to a maximum accrual of 40 hours per benefit year. Alternatively, employers may opt for an upfront "lump-sum" allocation of 40 hours at the beginning of each year to simplify administrative tracking.

  • Vesting: Employees begin accruing sick time on their first day of work and may begin utilizing it after 90 calendar days of employment.
  • Permissible uses: Medical care, illness, mental health, preventive doctor appointments for the employee or their child, spouse, parent, or parent-in-law.
  • Carryover: Up to 40 hours of unused earned sick time must roll over into the next benefit year unless the employer grants 40 hours upfront.

Strict Anti-Retaliation Safeguards

Massachusetts strictly prohibits taking adverse action, disciplinary points, or retaliating against an employee for exercising their right to earned sick time. Furthermore, employers may only request a doctor's note if an employee is absent for more than three consecutive workdays.
